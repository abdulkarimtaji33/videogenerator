# Viral video research: complete (long-form and Shorts)

Date: 2026-11-21. This file combines four earlier documents: `How Viral Videos Are Made`, `Phase Research - Long-form`, `Phase Research - Shorts` and `Phase Research - Hooks`. Where they disagreed, the later and better-supported finding is used, and the pipeline blueprint (section 8) is corrected accordingly. **The four originals are also included in full, verbatim, in the appendix (section 11), so no detail is lost.**

**Evidence labels:** [Official] read on YouTube's own pages. [Measured] counted from public channel data on 2026-11-21 (titles, views, upload age and duration of 46 established channels, 1,141 uploads; a few Shorts channels). I did not look inside any video. [Creator-stated] said by a creator in an interview or transcript I read. [Study] published research. [Practitioner] creator or vendor advice, weak evidence, often contradictory. [Inference] my reasoning. Hook sections also use [O] official, [C] creator or insider quote, [B] blog, [G] general pattern, [U] unverified.

**Key conclusions**
1. YouTube publishes no ideal length, no hook benchmark and no ranking weights. Its own signals: Intro retention at 30 seconds, the retention curve, watch-time share for title and thumbnail tests, and "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts.
2. **Title formulas show no measurable effect inside a channel** (numbers, questions, capitals, length). Topic and promise matter. Meta, announcement and compilation titles do worse.
3. **Length:** 30-60 minute videos did slightly best against channels' own norms; under 5 minutes did worst. Let the topic decide, avoid padding.
4. **Hook:** deliver the title's promise fast, no greeting or logo first, and vary hook types from video to video.
5. **Retention comes from story, not cuts:** link beats with "but" and "therefore", keep one to three open loops, add a side story at the halfway point, and change visuals when the idea changes (roughly every 10-30 seconds).
6. **Shorts:** visual hook first, hook about 3 seconds or less, a two-line foreshadow, payoff in the last seconds, then stop. Loops raise view counts, but earnings use engaged views, which exclude loops.
7. **Series and recurring characters are allowed, but identical repeated situations are ineligible.** Every episode must change the situation.
8. **Long-form and Shorts are separate products.** YouTube says viewers rarely cross formats, and Shorts earn nothing below 10M qualified views per 90 days from Feb 2027.
9. Most advice here is practitioner knowledge. There are no controlled studies for hooks, loops or pacing, and the numbers circulating online conflict.

## 1. What YouTube rewards

- **[Official]** The recommendation system tries to show each viewer videos they want to watch and to maximize their satisfaction over time. Rankings are personal and depend on context, such as device and time of day.
- **[Official]** YouTube's own summary of how to reach a larger audience is three things: come up with the idea, package it with a compelling title and thumbnail, and deliver value "all throughout" the video.
- **[Official]** "There's no universal ideal length." Use the exact length the content needs, avoid filler and artificial stretching, and read your retention curves to decide.
- **[Official]** Impressions depend on more than your own performance: competition from other videos, topic interest and seasonality. A good video can still get few impressions.
- **[Official]** The Shorts feed may weigh recency more heavily, which helps new content get discovered.
- **[Official]** Viewers rarely follow a creator across formats. YouTube says cross-format viewing "is not common behavior" and that preferences vary "not just by topic but also by format". Experimenting with Shorts does not hurt a long-form channel, but do not expect Shorts viewers to become long-form viewers. See section 8.
- **[Official]** Views are counted the moment a video starts to play, in every format. Partner Program earnings use "engaged" views and watch hours, and eligibility uses "qualified" ones.
- **[Practitioner, unverified]** Third-party guides quote "typical" click-through rates of 2–10% and say satisfaction surveys now outweigh raw watch time. I found no official source for exact weights or benchmarks. Compare against your own channel's history, not a universal number.

## 2. Long-form video

## 2.1 Packaging (title, thumbnail, promise)

### What YouTube says [Official]
- Test & Compare lets you test up to 3 titles and 3 thumbnails. YouTube picks the winner by **watch-time share, not click-through rate**, and warns that third-party tools which optimise click-through alone may pick a different winner.
- Results take a few days up to 2 weeks. Tests finish faster when the options differ more and when there are more impressions. YouTube suggests testing older videos first.
- Titles must be accurate: if the video does not match, viewers leave and discoverability suffers. Thumbnails should be uncluttered and as large as possible, 16:9 for custom thumbnails.
- The Intro metric (viewers still watching after 30 seconds) is higher when the first 30 seconds match what the title and thumbnail promised.

### What the data shows [Measured]
Across 1,141 uploads from 46 channels, title format barely matters inside a channel:

| Title feature | Median views vs channel norm | Videos above norm |
|---|---|---|
| 5 words or fewer / 6-9 / 10+ | 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 | 47% / 48% / 48% |
| Contains a number | 1.00 | 47% |
| Is a question | 1.00 | 50% |
| Starts with How or Why | 1.00 | 49% |
| ALL-CAPS word | 1.02 | 51% |
| Colon or dash | 1.04 | 52% |
| Stakes or superlative word (never, worst, secret, truth...) | 1.00 | 46% |
| First person (I, we, my) | 0.97 | 45% |
| **Series marker (Ep, S, Part, #)** | **0.88** | **38%** |
| **Announcement or meta words (update, trailer, podcast, compilation...)** | **0.89** | **38%** |
| Starts with What, Who, When, Where | 1.08 | 64% (only 33 videos, 18 channels: suggestive, not proven) |

- A "50% above norm" result means no effect. **Title tricks do not move views.** Only meta and announcement titles clearly lose.
- The Hindi/Urdu sample was too small to read (89 videos, 5 channels). Emoji was the only feature with a hint (1.17, 65% above, 20 videos), which is not enough to rely on.
- **Limits:** the sample is established channels, titles cannot capture the topic, and newer videos are still gaining views.

### What this means [Inference]
- The earlier top-versus-bottom comparison inside channels shows what separates winners: a **specific subject plus a stake or surprise** ("AI Just Crossed the Terrifying Line - Now What?" 24M against "We Made A New Channel!" 2.2M). That is a topic and promise effect, not a wording trick.
- So spend effort on **choosing the topic and promise**, then write several clearly different titles.

### Thumbnails [Practitioner and vendor study, weak]
- A vidIQ study of 500 breakout thumbnails found faces in 69% (80% of the top 50), high contrast in 56% (not rising with success), text in 72% with a median of 5 words, and no text in 28%. vidIQ sells thumbnail tools, so treat this with caution.
- A separate data report covered by Search Engine Journal found face and no-face thumbnails perform similarly on average, with multi-face thumbnails doing best and the effect varying by niche.
- No controlled study isolating face, contrast or text length was found. The eye-tracking claim that faces draw the eye first is cited second-hand and I could not find the original.
- For a faceless channel, a face is not required: Kurzgesagt, Fall of Civilizations and Mr. Nightmare succeed without on-camera people.

### Rules for the pipeline
1. Write the **promise** (one sentence the video delivers) before the script.
2. Produce **3 titles that differ in angle**, not in wording, because Test & Compare gives faster results when options differ more.
3. Produce 2-3 thumbnail concepts that differ visually: one focal subject, 3-5 words or none, high contrast, uncluttered.
4. The title and thumbnail must be delivered by the first 30 seconds.
5. Avoid meta titles ("update", "trailer", "compilation") for core content.
6. After publishing, run Test & Compare and judge by watch time, not click-through.

### Anatomy of the first 30 seconds
All timings below are general patterns [G] unless marked. No official source gives a second-by-second structure. Every specific number circulating online (such as a "3-second hold", "60% retention" or "81% swipe ratio") comes from blogs or single creators, not YouTube.

Official anchors [O]: grab attention by what you say or show, make the topic clear early, tease the rest, branding after the hook. The Intro metric is measured at 30 seconds.

| Time | Say | Show | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | The most striking, specific line from the video. No greeting. | The strongest visual, already moving | A greeting such as "Hey guys, welcome back" is called a retention killer by one blog [B] |
| 3-10 s | Deliver the title's promise in one sentence | A visual that matches the title and thumbnail | Intro success is tied to matching expectations [O] |
| 10-20 s | Add stakes or the first open loop (a question or a "but") | A change of image or motion | Blogs suggest teasing a later payoff [B] |
| 20-30 s | Bridge into the body and say what the viewer gets; foreshadow | The first body scene | Hoyos calls foreshadowing "the most underrated thing in hooks" [C, summary page] |

**What to cut [O/G]:** flashy logo stings, long channel intros, recaps of previous videos, "like and subscribe" in the first 30 seconds.

**MrBeast production guide [C, third-party copy, authenticity unverified]:** "The first minute of each video is the most important minute of each video." "Hook people at the start of the video, transition them to an amazing story that they are invested in." "The title and thumbnail ... set the expectations for the viewer." (Simon Willison's commentary adds that losing 21 million of 60 million viewers in the first minute is considered a good result; that is his remark, not the memo's text.)

## 2.3 Keeping people watching (beats, open loops, re-hooks)

### What YouTube says [Official]
- The retention report shows flat parts (watched start to finish), gradual declines, **spikes** (rewatching or sharing) and **dips** (skipping or leaving). "Top moments" are where almost nobody left.
- Videos generally taper off during playback. If your top moments come late, **introduce the compelling content earlier**. Make more content like your top moments, and review dips to see why interest dropped.
- You can compare against typical retention for your last 10 videos of similar length.
- No ideal length: avoid filler and artificial stretching.

### Why open loops work [Study]
- Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity (1994): curiosity arises when you notice a gap between what you know and what you want to know, and it feels like a deprivation you want to resolve. It needs some prior knowledge to notice the gap.
- Practical advice from a PBS/NOVA article drawing on it: start with a genuinely interesting question that opens a gap instead of giving the answer, and "prime the pump" with intriguing but incomplete information.
- Limits: rival accounts say curiosity peaks for novelty, and not every knowledge gap creates curiosity. The theory comes from lab and survey work, and **its application to video retention is an inference, not a tested result**.

### Editing and pacing [Practitioner, contradictory]
- Pattern interrupts (B-roll, graphics, zooms, music change, angle change) are the common tool. Recommended frequency ranges from every 10-15 seconds to every 30-60 seconds depending on the source.
- A single creator's controlled test found over-editing causes viewer fatigue, and B-roll helps only when it directly illustrates what is being said. Anecdotal.
- A vendor analysis of 100 long-form channels says top-quartile channels hold viewers 20-30% longer (methodology unchecked). Targets of about 50% average percentage viewed circulate widely and vary with video length.
- Coverage of a leaked internal MrBeast production guide says it treats the first minute as the most important and teaches "retention editing". The guide's origin is unconfirmed, and its creator says to take it with a grain of salt.
- **Nothing here could be measured from public data**, because retention graphs are private to each channel.

### Rules for the pipeline
1. Split the video into **beats of 45-90 seconds**, each with one claim, its evidence or source, and its visual.
2. A visual change at each beat start and when the idea shifts inside a beat; roughly every 10-30 seconds; every visual must illustrate the line being spoken.
3. Keep **one to three open loops running** at a time. Every opened loop is closed before the end, and the biggest is closed last.
4. Re-hook (restate the promise or open a new question) near one-third and two-thirds of the video. These positions are starting hypotheses, not proven numbers.
5. Place the strongest moment in the first third.
6. After publishing, read the retention graph: fix the largest dip first, and copy what created the spikes.

## 2.4 Length and format that performed

### What YouTube says [Official]
There is no universal ideal length. Use the exact length the content needs. Avoid filler. Use retention curves to decide.

### What the data shows [Measured]
1,141 uploads, 46 channels, views divided by each channel's own median.

| Length | Videos | Channels | Median vs channel norm | Videos above norm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-5 min | 22 | 9 | 0.66 | 23% |
| 5-10 min | 110 | 21 | 0.99 | 49% |
| 10-15 min | 149 | 26 | 0.92 | 39% |
| 15-20 min | 223 | 34 | 1.00 | 49% |
| 20-30 min | 336 | 35 | 1.00 | 46% |
| **30-60 min** | **201** | **31** | **1.15** | **60%** |
| 60+ min | 100 | 21 | 1.00 | 46% |

- Very short long-form videos (1-5 min) clearly underperform. 30-60 minutes does slightly best. The middle lengths are all near the norm. The differences are modest.
- Best length bucket per channel varies widely; most land between 15 and 60 minutes. Several single-channel results are based on only 4-5 videos and are noisy.
- Hindi/Urdu titles: 78 videos from 4 channels, 5-10 min median 1.71 (15 videos) and 10-15 min 1.00. Too small to rely on.

### Format patterns seen on real channels [Measured, small]
- A strict repeatable title format gives steady views (Mr. Nightmare: "3 Scary TRUE ... Stories", 650K-1.1M each, 23-29 minutes).
- Core episodes beat compilations and podcasts on the same channel (Khooni Monday: 11-12 minute episodes 1.1-1.6M; 47-minute compilation about 1.2M; 76-minute podcast 248K).
- Landmark videos can be very long: Fall of Civilizations makes 4-7 hour documentaries (47 videos in five years, 2.2M median).
- **Policy caution:** a rigid template with thin new content is what YouTube's "generic or repetitive" category targets. The format can repeat; the content must be new and sourced each time.

### Watch hours [Inference, assumed viewing time]
Views needed for the 8,000-hour entry bar: about 107K for a 10-minute video, 48K for 25 minutes, 19K for one hour. Longer videos need far fewer views but cost more footage.

### Production load [Inference]
Veoframe Pro quota is 120 eight-second clips per day. A 30-minute video that is 40% AI clips needs about 90 clips, so roughly one such video per day at most. Stills with motion, maps and charts carry the rest.

### Rules for the pipeline
1. Default to **20-40 minutes** for core videos; allow 10-20 minutes for simple topics; avoid under 5 minutes for long-form.
2. Let the topic set the length. Do not pad (official guidance).
3. Keep a repeatable title and episode format, with different content each time.
4. Avoid compilations and podcast formats as core output.
5. Budget AI clips per video against the daily quota.

## 2.5 Ending

### What YouTube says [Official]
- End screens can be placed in the last 5-20 seconds of a video that is at least 25 seconds long. Up to four elements. Viewers can hide them. Cards are hidden while an end screen shows.
- YouTube advises keeping overlays from covering visuals viewers want to see, and checking the "End screen element click rate" in Analytics.
- For binge watching, YouTube's TV tips suggest grouping episodes into playlists with a consistent title format.
- Viewers keep watching a session until a "Continue watching?" prompt appears (after 30 minutes on mobile, 60 on web, 180 on TV of uninterrupted autoplay).

### What sources say [Practitioner]
- Deliver the promised payoff, then bridge to a related video. Cut long outros. No source measured this.

### Inference
Retention tapers over the video (official), so by the end only the most engaged viewers remain. The ending matters less for reach and more for the next video and subscribers.

### Rules for the pipeline
1. Deliver the **payoff the title promised** before anything else.
2. A **10-20 second bridge**: name one specific next video and why it follows.
3. No long sign-off, thanks or recap beyond one line.
4. Keep the final visual clean for the end screen; place 1-2 elements (best next video, subscribe) in the last 5-20 seconds.
5. Put episodes in playlists with a consistent title format.
6. Review end-screen click rate and the final retention segment after publishing.

## 2.6 What top creators and researchers have shared

Written sources only (interview transcripts, creator blogs, published research). Nothing here was checked inside a video.

### Colin & Samir interview with MrBeast [Creator-stated, transcript read]
- **Re-engage halfway.** "It's very hard with a single storyline if you're doing a double-digit-minute video to have that one thing drip their entire attention... typically if you're doing a longer video, you should introduce a side story and have some plan halfway through to re-engage them." A single chase cannot fill 15 minutes.
- **Count what happens in the first 30 seconds.** Samir described writing down a video's first 30 seconds and finding more than 12 scene changes, new information about every six seconds, and a twist by 40 seconds, with tension running throughout.
- **Content over packaging.** "If you're frantically changing thumbnails afterwards, you're already way too far gone." They only publish when it feels like "a banger", and treat the title and thumbnail as a test of whether the video can back up its promise.
- **Making the title and thumbnail first** forces you to clarify the core premise (from The Futur's summary of the same interview).
- Colin & Samir's playbook summarises their three rules as: if they don't click, they don't watch; respect their time; do you want more stuff like this. They also say they track first-seven-second and first-30-second viewership. [Playbook page and a second-hand summary]

### Story mechanics [Creator-stated and Study]
- **"But / therefore" instead of "and then"** (Trey Parker and Matt Stone, NYU 2014). Write the beats as a list and read the gaps. Wherever the link is only "and then", a viewer can leave without missing anything. Creators call this the single most useful pass for catching retention dips before editing.
- **Open loops and the Zeigarnik effect.** Zeigarnik's 1927 work found people remember unfinished tasks better than finished ones; creators use this to explain why an early question keeps viewers watching. Advanced creators stack loops so something is always pending. The step from lab result to video retention is an inference.
- **Information-gap theory** (Loewenstein, 1994): curiosity comes from noticing a gap between what you know and want to know, so a good hook gives enough to see the gap without the answer.
- **Sharing psychology (Berger's STEPPS):** social currency, triggers, emotion, public visibility, practical value, stories. Berger notes strongly sad content is shared less than average, even if remembered. Applies to share-driven growth, not retention.

### How a top explainer channel actually works [Creator-stated, second-hand summaries]
- Kurzgesagt says a roughly 10-minute video takes at least 1,200 hours: reading books and papers and consulting experts first, scripts rewritten many times and reviewed by outside experts, then storyboarding, several weeks of illustration and animation, and narration recorded last. The script is the bottleneck. This is the opposite of an automated pipeline, so the lesson is that **research and a rewritten script, not visuals, are where quality comes from**.

### Published research on video length [Study]
- A study of 6.9 million edX course-video sessions (Guo, Kim and Rubin, 2014) found engagement dropped sharply after about six minutes. That is educational lecture video, not YouTube entertainment, and conflicts with the 30-60 minute result in my measured data above. **Do not apply the six-minute figure to YouTube documentaries.** It does support keeping each beat short and varied.

### Updated rules from the deeper research
1. Add a **planned side story or new question at the halfway point** (MrBeast).
2. In the first 30 seconds, aim for **frequent new information** (about one new piece every 6 seconds in his example) and a **twist or escalation by about 40 seconds**. This describes one famous channel, not a law.
3. Run the **"but / therefore" check on the beat list before any visuals are made**; rewrite any beat linked only by "and then".
4. Treat **research and the script as the main quality investment**; Kurzgesagt's own hours are spent there.
5. Decide whether to publish with the gut-check from the interview: if you would not call it a banger, rework it.

## 3.2 One idea (20-45 seconds)

### What YouTube says [Official]
- Shorts can be up to 3 minutes. The Shorts feed may weigh recency more, which helps new content get discovered.
- Studio shows "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts. Compare it against your own channel's baseline.
- In a YouTube blog interview (Aug 2025), creator Jordan Howlett said his best signal is the early reaction: "I usually know within the first hour if the video is going to do well", judged by the ratio of likes to views. He also said trends are a launchpad for new creators, but original ideas last longer.

### What top Shorts creators say [Creator-stated]
From the Creator Science podcast interview with Jenny Hoyos (an 18-year-old creator the show says is averaging about 10M views per Short):
- **A good Short has a strong hook, one that could work as the title and thumbnail of a long video and still get clicks.** It should be visual enough to understand without sound, and so simple you could explain it to a five-year-old.
- **She checks hook wording in a readability checker** and aims for fifth-grade level or lower.
- **A good Short pushes you to the end and makes you rewatch.** She says 90%+ average retention is "the bare minimum" for a Short to blow up, and that this level requires viewers to rewatch. Her own average is about 95% on videos with around 10M views.
- **Length:** her own analysis says her most popular videos are about 34 seconds, so she aims for that. She stresses that everyone's number is different and has to be analysed per channel.
- **Process:** a rough script before filming, only to estimate length from the word count. She always writes a hook, then a **foreshadow** of two lines (about 3 seconds or less), then the body. Ideas are narrowed from a long list to about 25, then to about 10 with her editor, who acts as a strategist.
- **Closing rule:** "Whatever you say you're gonna do, you end it right after you do it." Nothing trails after the payoff.
- She sketches the hook as if it were a long-form thumbnail before deciding the words.

### What guides and newsletters say [Practitioner, weak]
- Common four-part model: hook, core, payoff, then loop or call to action. Without a payoff the video feels unfinished.
- One guide: plan the opening three seconds and the final three seconds first, and treat everything between as a bridge. If the ending cannot answer the first question, the idea is probably a topic, not a story.
- Length advice conflicts: 15-25 s for tutorials, 20-40 s as a "safe default", 25-35 s for most viral Shorts. One vendor analysis claims only 2% of Shorts view variation correlates with length. Choose length by goal, not by rule.
- **"But / therefore" rule:** Trey Parker and Matt Stone (South Park) told an NYU audience in 2014 that if the link between two beats is "and then", something is wrong; it should be "but" (conflict) or "therefore" (consequence). Creators apply it to scripts, and Hoyos is reported to use it in her middle section.
- Colin & Samir's paid playbook names a "Stop, Hook, Payoff" method for Shorts from a guest creator. Its contents are behind a paywall, so I cannot describe it.

### My measured notes [Measured, earlier work]
- The strongest Shorts titles in the channels studied were one-line curiosity questions or story hooks. Channel promotions got the least.

### Rules for the pipeline [Inference from the sources]
1. **One promise per Short**, stated in one sentence a child could repeat.
2. Write the **hook visual first**, then the words. Check the hook text for reading level.
3. Structure: **hook (about 1-3 s), foreshadow (2 lines, about 3 s), body linked by "but" and "therefore", payoff in the last seconds, then stop.**
4. Start with **25-40 seconds** and let the channel's own analytics set the final length.
5. For AI clips: a 30-40 second Short needs about 3-5 clips of 8 seconds, or fewer clips plus stills with motion.
6. Compare each Short's "viewed vs swiped away" with the channel's own baseline.

## 3.3 Loop ending

### What YouTube says [Official]
- A Shorts view counts each time it starts to play or replays, with no minimum watch time (a change dated Mar 31, 2025). A later help page says views are counted the moment a video starts to play in all formats from Aug 24, 2026. The two pages are worded differently, so check the live page if this matters.
- **"Engaged views" do not include loops.** Partner Program eligibility and Shorts earnings use engaged views, not raw views.
- So a loop can raise public view counts without raising what counts for pay. [Inference from the two official facts above.]

### What creators say [Creator-stated and Practitioner]
- Hoyos links rewatching to retention above 90%. Her tactic is to **give the viewer an expectation, close it, then add a twist** (example she gave: she shows she will give someone a gift, then the gift breaks).
- vidIQ describes a seamless loop as hiding the join between the last frame and the first. A brand guide suggests filming the ending shot first and the opening shot to match it.
- Cautions in the same sources: looping does not guarantee double watch time, and over-trimming a Short to two or three seconds rarely builds real watch time.
- Another source warns that an open curiosity loop with no payoff earns swipe-backs and "not interested" taps, not satisfaction.

### Rules for the pipeline [Inference]
1. **Payoff first, loop second.** The viewer must get what the hook promised before any loop trick.
2. Make the **last line or frame connect to the first** so a replay feels natural, but do not delay the payoff to do it.
3. Do not rely on loops for income: engaged views exclude them.
4. Track average percentage viewed. Values above 100% mean rewatching.
5. Avoid cliffhanger endings with no resolution; they are the "unsatisfying" kind of content that policy warns about.

## 3.4 Recurring character or series

### What YouTube says [Official]
- **Shorts series:** creators can organise Shorts into seasons and episodes with custom thumbnails and sequential playback; members can continue straight to the next episode.
- **Microdramas (YouTube blog, Oct 2026):** serialised vertical mini-series told in fast 60-second scenes passed **6.5 billion views in the first half of 2026**, with watch time growing more than 50% year over year. About **40% of views and 30% of clicks came more than 30 days after publishing**, so early episodes keep finding viewers. Examples named: Japan's GOKKO CLUB (emotional hook within 15 seconds, vertical-first framing) and India's Shorts Break (high-frequency publishing, universal themes, audience comments shaping future characters).
- For binge watching, YouTube's TV tips suggest playlists and a consistent title format per episode.
- **Policy line [Official, monetization policy page]:** content that puts "characters in the same situation over and over again with the same outcome" is listed as repetitive and ineligible, while using AI "to visualize a unique character and narrative you invented" is listed as acceptable. A recurring character is fine; a repeated identical scenario is not.

### What creators and guides say
- **Neural Viz [secondary summaries of a WIRED profile, not the creator's own words]:** writes scripts by hand and storyboards shots, uses several tools to keep lighting and sight lines consistent, performs the characters himself using motion capture ("wearing AI as a mask"), and builds multiple series in one shared world.
- A creator-strategy guide frames a series as **character, quest, tension and a repeatable format**. It notes comedic character series suffer format fatigue faster than explainer series, and that the real metric is whether viewers return, not raw views.
- Another guide says to make the one recurring element a person (a character), not a product shot. Marketing blogs; no data.

### My measured notes [Measured, earlier work]
- Bandar Apna Dost (AI-made) uses named recurring characters and melodrama and has Shorts up to 144M views, but its worst Shorts got about 48K, a 3,000-times spread, and its policy exposure is high.
- In long-form titles across 46 channels, videos marked as series parts ("Part 2", "Ep 12") did slightly **worse** than the same channels' other videos (median 0.87 of channel norm, 33 videos, 8 channels). Later episodes of a series often get fewer views than the first. Series help return viewers, not each video's views.

### Rules for the pipeline [Inference from the sources]
1. Build a **character bible** (look, voice, goal, flaw) and a **world bible** that every episode reuses, so visuals stay consistent.
2. Each episode must **change the situation**: new problem, new "but", new consequence. Never repeat the same scenario with the same outcome.
3. Plan arcs across episodes (seasons) and keep one repeatable format and title pattern.
4. Use YouTube's Shorts series feature or playlists, and number episodes consistently.
5. Track **returning viewers and subscribers gained** per Short, not just views.
6. Treat recurring-character comedy as the highest-risk format for format fatigue and for the repetitive-content policy.

## 3.5 Patterns measured on real Shorts channels

**[Measured]** What the top Shorts in four channels have in common:
- **Bandar Apna Dost (AI-made):** top Shorts 144M, 90M, 64M, 62M views, but the worst of its latest 48 got only 48K, a 3,000x spread. The winners use named recurring characters (Halku, Boltu, Bablu), melodrama or slapstick, and an emotional or patriotic hook in the title. The channel's long-form videos get a median of 80K and the last long upload was about a year ago, so this is a Shorts-only success.
- **Kurzgesagt Shorts:** one-line curiosity questions as titles ("Why Does Cocaine Feel So Good?" 4.3M; "The Deadliest Thing in Your Kitchen" 5.3M). Median Shorts 1.4M against a long-form median of 5.65M.
- **Mr. Nightmare Shorts:** story-hook titles in the first person ("Girl Home Alone Hears Knocks at the Door... Then This Happens"), best 1.4M, median 289K against a long-form median of 993K.
- **Khooni Monday Shorts:** place-based story titles ("Indore Girl Possession Story!") best 144K, but promos for live events only 12–18K. Median Shorts 43.5K against long-form 829K.

**Pattern across the four:** the best Shorts have either a recurring character or world, or a one-line curiosity or story hook that is clear in the first second. The worst are channel promotions.

**[Official]** Studio shows "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts. Compare it against your own channel's baseline.

**[Practitioner]** Hook in the first 1–2 seconds on screen and in audio, one idea per Short, large captions, a last line that loops back to the first, and a series structure.

## 4. Hook types, library and checklist (both formats)

### Taxonomy of hook types

Names and sample lines come from vidIQ, Opus and Social Media Examiner (John Scott of HookBomb). All [B] unless stated. **Sample lines are the blogs' own illustrations, not real video openings.**

| Type | How it works | Sourced sample line | Fits | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Curiosity / unanswered question | Opens a gap the viewer wants closed | "One video changed everything for me, but it wasn't the one I expected." (vidIQ). Shorts: "Why do your Shorts die at 40% watch time?" (Opus) | Explainers, mystery | Vague question, or answer given too soon |
| In medias res | Starts mid-moment, skips setup | "It's 2am. I just lost 43 subscribers in one hour. I made a huge mistake." (vidIQ) | Story, horror, true crime | Context never delivered |
| Relatable problem | Names the viewer's pain and promises a fix | "Your YouTube intros are putting people to sleep. Here's why." (vidIQ) | How-to, finance | Generic "you" claims |
| Direct / time promise | Specific outcome plus time frame | "In the next 60 seconds, I'll show you the thumbnail strategy that doubled my click-through rate." (vidIQ). Shorts: "60-second pasta that actually tastes good." (Opus) | Tutorial, food | Overpromise |
| Raise the stakes | Something is at risk | "If this video doesn't hit 10,000 views, I'm quitting YouTube." (vidIQ) | Challenge, personal | Fake stakes lose trust |
| Contrarian / myth-bust | Challenges common advice | "Stop asking people to subscribe." (vidIQ); "Longer Shorts actually get more views, and I'll prove it in 30 seconds." (Opus) | Education, opinion | No proof follows |
| Visual hook | Striking image before words | vidIQ gives descriptions only and warns "weird is fine, but pointless is fatal." | Any visual niche | Image unrelated to the topic |
| Shocking statistic | A surprising number, then the reason | "Only 6.6% of YouTube channels ever reach 1,000 subscribers." (vidIQ) | Business, science | Unverifiable stat; a factual-risk point for a faceless channel [G] |
| Numbered / listicle | Count promised up front | "3 settings that fix blurry Shorts" (Opus) | Tutorial Shorts | Unclear count |
| Pattern interrupt | Two surprising elements together | "Somebody keeps ripping my for-sale signs out of the yards. So I bought a baseball bat." (Scott) | Story Shorts | Surprise with no payoff |
| Regret opener | Signals an avoidable obstacle | "I wish I had known this before I [action]." (Scott) | Advice Shorts | Overused |
| Foreshadowed payoff | States what is coming but withholds it | Hoyos: "here are three reasons why X" [C, paraphrase from a summary page] | All | Stated, then never delivered |
| Cold open on the strongest moment | Shows the best bit, then rewinds | The Playbook's "sneak peek of the finished product" [O]; a "wow factor" example from the MrBeast guide copy [C/U] | Challenge, build | Never tied back in |
| Text-based subtext | On-screen phrase adds a second layer; Scott calls text the most underused hook | "What marketers say to their clients vs. what they want to say." (Scott) | Shorts | Unreadable or too long |

Open-loop idea: an unanswered question holds attention (repeated by Scott and many blogs). The underlying paper was not fetched here, so treat it as [G]. The wider research on curiosity gaps is in sections 2.3 and 2.6.

### Hook checklist

1. Does the first line contain a specific claim, number, scene or question, not a greeting?
2. Does the first visual match the title and thumbnail? [O]
3. Is the title's promise stated within about 10 seconds (long-form) or in the first line (Shorts)? [G]
4. Is there one open loop, and is it paid off?
5. Is branding after the hook? [O]
6. Does the Short make sense on mute? [C]
7. Are AI visuals free of text, face and hand artifacts in the first 3 seconds? [G]
8. Is the narration line short enough for TTS to land the key noun early? [G]
9. Is this hook formula different from the last several videos' hooks (policy risk)? [O/G]
10. Is every number or claim verified? [G]

### Hook failure modes

- Title and hook mismatch, which lowers Intro [O].
- Buried lede: the point arrives after the viewer has left.
- Fake or unresolved open loop.
- Flashy intro or logo first [O].
- An unverified statistic spoken with confidence by the narrator.
- The same hook template reused across videos [O].
- Text artifacts or distorted faces in the opening frame [G].
- A looping Short that inflates views but not engaged views [O].

## 5. Emotional levers and the policy line

- **[Practitioner]** What makes people watch, rewatch and share: curiosity, surprise or awe, stakes, identity ("this is about people like me"), practical value, humor, nostalgia.
- **[Official, confirmed this session]** YouTube's monetization policy rewards "a unique creative voice" and "using AI to visualize a unique character and narrative you invented". It excludes content that is mass-produced, generic or repetitive, and "emotionally manipulative formulas" or content that "mimics existing formats ... to a degree that the videos feel interchangeable". Examples listed as off-putting include repeated scenarios of animals in distress, and videos that "stitch together unrelated or inconsistent AI clips to surprise or shock viewers".
- **[Inference]** Bandar Apna Dost's formula (the same characters in repeated melodramatic scenarios) sits close to this line. Its monetization status is unknown to me, so do not treat its views as proof the format is safe.
- **[Inference]** Use the levers that add real value (curiosity answered with sourced facts, a new angle, real stakes), not the ones that only manufacture emotion.

## 6. Production levers and AI visuals

- **[Practitioner]** Editing rhythm, sound design, music under narration, clear voice delivery, and captions all affect retention.
- **[Inference for our pipeline]**
  - **Where AI visuals help:** abstract or impossible subjects (space, deep time, microscopic or historical reconstructions, "what if" scenes). Nobody expects real footage there, so small imperfections matter less.
  - **Where they hurt:** close-up human faces and hands, readable text inside the picture, and anything that must look like real documentary footage.
  - **Consistency:** reuse one character reference and one style prefix, as the Video Generator already does for characters.
  - **Narration:** a synthetic voice with natural pacing; vary sentence length; avoid a monotone read. Test the voice by ear before committing.
  - **Mix of visuals:** use AI clips for the hook and key beats, then stills with motion, maps, charts and text cards for the rest, so each 8–15 second clip is spent where it counts.

### Hooks, visuals and synthetic voice

**Policy first [O].** YouTube's monetization page renamed "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content" and lists as non-monetizable:
- "Videos that use a highly similar storyline template across multiple videos."
- "Image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal narrative or educational value."
- "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates that gives the impression of mass production."

It allows "the same intro and outro across videos, if the bulk of each video differs."

Implication [G]: **rotate hook formulas and structure.** Never output the same hook skeleton with swapped nouns every time. Official wording on how YouTube treats AI voice specifically was not found.

**Visual choices**
- An impossible or cinematic image suits Veo-style clips as a cold open, because AI can show what cameras cannot. The Playbook supports a "stunning visual" opening [O].
- Google's Veo best-practice documentation [O]: "dedicate each prompt to a single, focused moment"; chaining events "often leads to muddled or incomplete videos"; for character consistency, build "a rich set of unchangeable features"; avoid quotation marks, which can trigger text rendering.
- Text artifacts: generate clips without text and add on-screen text in the editor [G]. How bad text artifacts are in current Veo versions: [U].
- Faces: no official guidance found [U]. A cautious rule: avoid close-up faces, hands and crowds in the first 3 seconds, where artifacts would show most [G].
- Match the hook image to the title. A mismatch directly harms Intro [O].

**Synthetic voice (ElevenLabs documentation, one vendor only [O])**
- Punctuation affects delivery; ellipses add pauses and weight.
- Break tags up to 3 seconds; too many can cause instability.
- Speed range 0.7 to 1.2.
- Expand abbreviations and write numbers in spoken form.
- Other TTS engines differ [U].

Hook-line writing rules for TTS [G]: one idea per sentence, under about 12 words; the key noun early; one comma-pause before the reveal; no numerals the engine may misread.

**Urdu and Hindi**
- The ElevenLabs page has no Hindi or Urdu guidance.
- A Pakistan guide [B, single source, no data] claims Nastaliq thumbnails suit local audiences and tags should include Roman Urdu. Untested.
- **No sourced Hindi or Urdu hook examples or retention data were found.** A Hindi template the search surfaced, "Kya aap jaante hain ki [fact]?", was the search tool's own suggestion, not from a source. Use it only as a hypothesis to test [U].
- Keep Hindi (Devanagari) and Urdu (Nastaliq) libraries separate [G]. Preferred caption script and mixing ratio: [U].

## 8. Blueprint for the pipeline

### 8.1 Long-form scene-plan template (what Claude writes per video)
1. `promise`: one sentence the video must deliver. Write it first.
2. `titles`: 3 options that differ in **angle**, not wording. `thumbnail_concepts`: 2-3 that differ visually (one focal subject, 3-5 words or none, high contrast, uncluttered).
3. `hook` (0-30 s): 0-3 s the most striking, specific line and image, no greeting or logo; by about 10 s state the title's promise; 10-20 s stakes or the first open loop; 20-30 s bridge into the body. **Choose a different hook type from the last several videos.**
4. `beats[]` of 45-90 s: `claim`, `evidence_or_source`, `visual` (ai_video / still_motion / map / chart / card), `link_to_next` (must be "but" or "therefore", never "and then"), `loop_opened`, `loop_closed`.
5. Rules: one to three open loops running at any time; a **side story or new question at the halfway point**; re-hook near one-third and two-thirds (starting hypotheses); the strongest moment in the first third; change the visual when the idea changes (roughly every 10-30 s), never decoratively; a source for every factual claim.
6. `payoff` that delivers exactly what the title promised, then a 10-20 s bridge naming one specific next video; no long outro; clean final visual for the end screen (last 5-20 s).
7. Length: let the topic decide, default **20-40 minutes**, range **15-60**; avoid under 5 minutes; no padding.
8. Metadata: description with sources and chapters, tags, synthetic-media label where realistic, episode in a playlist with a consistent title format.

### 8.2 Short scene-plan template
1. `hook_visual` first (understandable on mute, simple enough for a child), then `hook_line` (about 3 s or less, reading level checked).
2. `foreshadow`: two lines, about 3 s, promising where it goes.
3. Body linked with "but" and "therefore"; `payoff` in the last seconds; **stop right after the payoff**.
4. `loop`: last line or frame connects to the first, without delaying the payoff.
5. Start at **25-40 s** and tune to the channel's own analytics. A 30-40 s Short is about 3-5 AI clips of 8 s, or fewer clips plus stills with motion.
6. Series: a character bible and world bible; every episode changes the situation; consistent title and episode numbering; track returning viewers.

### 8.3 Pre-publish QA checklist
1. The first line is a specific claim, number, scene or question, not a greeting.
2. The first visual matches the title and thumbnail, and the promise is delivered inside 30 seconds (long-form) or the first line (Shorts).
3. Every beat passes the "but / therefore" check; there is at least one open loop and it is paid off.
4. Branding comes after the hook.
5. The Short makes sense on mute.
6. AI visuals are free of text, face and hand artifacts, especially in the first 3 seconds.
7. Narration lines are short, with the key noun early, and no numerals the TTS may misread.
8. Every number and claim has a source.
9. Hook, structure and topic differ from the last several videos (inauthentic-content policy).
10. Not built on distress, shock or a manufactured emotional formula; the situation changes between episodes.

### 8.4 Failure modes (measured and sourced)
- Videos about the channel itself, announcements, compilations and format experiments (lowest views in every channel measured).
- Title and hook mismatch, which lowers Intro retention.
- A rigid template with thin new content, or the same character in the same situation with the same outcome (policy risk).
- Fake or unresolved open loops; a loop that inflates view counts but not engaged views.
- Padding videos to hit a length.
- Large subscriber counts with collapsed current views.

### 8.5 Metrics to watch (thresholds are rules of thumb, not YouTube numbers)
- **Intro retention (30 s)**, compared with the channel's own typical band in Studio.
- **Click-through rate** against the channel's own median; use Test & Compare and judge by **watch time**, not click-through.
- **Average view duration and the retention curve shape**: fix the largest dip first; copy what created the spikes.
- **Shorts viewed vs swiped away** against the channel's own baseline; average percentage viewed above 100% means rewatching.
- **Subscribers gained by content type** (videos vs Shorts) and **returning viewers**.

### 8.6 What this means for the plan
1. **Long-form and Shorts are separate products** with separate success numbers. YouTube says viewers rarely cross formats.
2. **Shorts do not monetize below 10M qualified Shorts views per 90 days** (from Feb 1, 2027). Treat Shorts as reach experiments, not income.
3. **Long-form is the route to the 8,000 watch hours** (or 20M Shorts views for new applicants).
4. **Research and the script are the main quality investment**, not visuals.
5. Vary hooks, structure and topics deliberately; the policy targets templated, mass-produced content.

### Appendix A: How a viral YouTube video is made: parts, methods, and a blueprint for our pipeline

Date: 2026-11-21. Written for the automated, faceless AI video channel (Claude writes the plan; AI clips, stills, TTS and captions are assembled).

**How to read the evidence labels**
- **[Official]** read directly on YouTube's own pages today.
- **[Measured]** taken from real channels on 2026-11-21 with a script that reads public channel pages. Small samples (7 long-form channels, 4 Shorts channels). It shows patterns, not proof of cause.
- **[Practitioner]** common advice from creator-education blogs. Weak evidence, often contradictory. Use as a starting hypothesis, then test.
- **[Inference]** my own reasoning from the above.

Nobody can engineer virality. Real data shows the same channel's videos varying by 60x (long-form) to about 3,000x (Shorts). What you can do is raise the odds on every video and run many honest experiments.

---

#### 1. What YouTube actually rewards

- **[Official]** The recommendation system tries to show each viewer videos they want to watch and to maximize their satisfaction over time. Rankings are personal and depend on context, such as device and time of day.
- **[Official]** YouTube's own summary of how to reach a larger audience is three things: come up with the idea, package it with a compelling title and thumbnail, and deliver value "all throughout" the video.
- **[Official]** "There's no universal ideal length." Use the exact length the content needs, avoid filler and artificial stretching, and read your retention curves to decide.
- **[Official]** Impressions depend on more than your own performance: competition from other videos, topic interest and seasonality. A good video can still get few impressions.
- **[Official]** The Shorts feed may weigh recency more heavily, which helps new content get discovered.
- **[Official]** Viewers rarely follow a creator across formats. YouTube says cross-format viewing "is not common behavior" and that preferences vary "not just by topic but also by format". Experimenting with Shorts does not hurt a long-form channel, but do not expect Shorts viewers to become long-form viewers. **Section 7 explains why this changes our plan.**
- **[Official]** Views are counted the moment a video starts to play, in every format. Partner Program earnings use "engaged" views and watch hours, and eligibility uses "qualified" ones.
- **[Practitioner, unverified]** Third-party guides quote "typical" click-through rates of 2–10% and say satisfaction surveys now outweigh raw watch time. I found no official source for exact weights or benchmarks. Compare against your own channel's history, not a universal number.

#### 2. Anatomy of a high-performing long-form video

##### 2.1 Packaging comes first: title, thumbnail and the promise
- **[Official]** The "Intro" metric is the share of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds. YouTube says a high intro score can mean the first 30 seconds matched what the title and thumbnail promised. Its advice for a low score is to make the title and thumbnail reflect the actual content.
- **[Measured]** Across 7 channels, the best-performing recent videos all share one thing: one concrete subject plus a stake or a surprise.
  - Kurzgesagt: "AI Just Crossed the Terrifying Line - Now What?" 24M views; "This Woman Cured Her Cancer with an Insane Method" 21M; "We Finally Know More About Humanity's First Civilization" 12M.
  - Wendover Productions: "How the Channel Tunnel Works" 2.3M; "How Taiwan is Preparing to Get Invaded" 1.8M; "How Red Bull Built Its Empire" 1.7M.
  - SciMyth (Hindi): a scale/speed claim ("3 crore times faster than light") 2.4M against typical videos of 30–120K.
- **[Measured]** The weakest recent videos in the same channels are about the channel, not the subject, or are format experiments: Kurzgesagt "We Made A New Channel!" and "This Game Took Us Two Years To Make" (715K); Wendover "One Piece of News From Every Country" (268K, 38 min); Khooni Monday's announcement videos (14K–76K).
- **[Practitioner]** Write the title and thumbnail concept before the script, so the video is built to deliver the promise. Thumbnail text of about 3–5 words, one focal subject, high contrast.

##### 2.2 The hook (first 5–30 seconds)
- **[Official]** Retention "tapers off" over the length of any video, and if your best moments occur late, introduce the compelling content earlier.
- **[Practitioner]** Restate the promise in the first seconds, show the most striking visual first (a "cold open"), state the stakes, and end the hook on an open question. Skip logos, long intros and "welcome back". Sources disagree on the window (8 s vs 15 s vs 30 s), so treat all as the same idea: no slow start.

##### 2.3 Keeping people watching
- **[Official]** The retention report shows flat sections (people watch start to finish), gradual declines, spikes (rewatching or sharing) and dips (skipping). "Top moments" are where almost nobody left. YouTube's advice: study the top moments and make more like them, and review dips to see why interest fell.
- **[Practitioner]** Techniques creators use:
  - **Open loops:** raise a question early, answer it later, and open a smaller one before closing the last.
  - **Re-hooks:** refresh the promise around the one-third and two-thirds marks.
  - **Beats of 45–90 seconds** that each make one claim, give the evidence, and change the visual.
  - **Visual change every few seconds,** with the pattern-break coming from the content, not just a cut.
  - **Escalation:** each section raises the stakes or the strangeness.
- **[Inference]** These all work by one rule: never leave the viewer without a reason to keep watching for the next 10 seconds.

##### 2.4 Length and format that actually performed
| Channel | What won (measured) |
|---|---|
| Kurzgesagt | 12–22 minute single-subject videos, 5–24M views |
| Wendover | 17–24 minute "How/Why" explainers, 0.55–2.3M |
| Mr. Nightmare | A strict template: "3 Scary TRUE [theme] Horror Stories", 23–29 min, 0.65–1.1M each, very steady |
| Khooni Monday (Hindi) | 11–12 minute episodes 1.1–1.6M; 47-minute compilations about 1.2M; a 76-minute podcast 248K |
| Dekho Suno Jano (Urdu) | Series episodes with a question title, 22–27 min, 136–371K; 2.5-hour season compilation 96K |
| SciMyth (Hindi) | 12–16 minute videos, 11K–2.4M; the extreme scale/speed claims won |
| Fall of Civilizations | 4–7 hour documentaries, one at 15M; only 47 videos in 5 years |

- **[Measured]** The 12–24 minute single-subject video is the most common winner. Very long videos can work, but only for landmark productions. Compilations and podcast-style uploads usually do worse than the core format.
- **[Measured]** Consistency works: a clear, repeatable title format (Mr. Nightmare, Khooni Monday episode numbers, Dekho Suno Jano season/episode) gives steady views. **But** a rigid template is exactly what YouTube's "generic or repetitive" category targets, so the template must carry different original content each time (see section 4).

##### 2.5 Ending
- **[Practitioner]** Deliver the payoff you promised, then bridge to a related video. Cut the long outro. [Inference] Retention is the signal that matters most at the end, so a clean finish beats a padded one.

#### 3. Anatomy of a high-performing Short

**[Measured]** What the top Shorts in four channels have in common:
- **Bandar Apna Dost (AI-made):** top Shorts 144M, 90M, 64M, 62M views, but the worst of its latest 48 got only 48K, a 3,000x spread. The winners use named recurring characters (Halku, Boltu, Bablu), melodrama or slapstick, and an emotional or patriotic hook in the title. The channel's long-form videos get a median of 80K and the last long upload was about a year ago, so this is a Shorts-only success.
- **Kurzgesagt Shorts:** one-line curiosity questions as titles ("Why Does Cocaine Feel So Good?" 4.3M; "The Deadliest Thing in Your Kitchen" 5.3M). Median Shorts 1.4M against a long-form median of 5.65M.
- **Mr. Nightmare Shorts:** story-hook titles in the first person ("Girl Home Alone Hears Knocks at the Door... Then This Happens"), best 1.4M, median 289K against a long-form median of 993K.
- **Khooni Monday Shorts:** place-based story titles ("Indore Girl Possession Story!") best 144K, but promos for live events only 12–18K. Median Shorts 43.5K against long-form 829K.

**Pattern across the four:** the best Shorts have either a recurring character or world, or a one-line curiosity or story hook that is clear in the first second. The worst are channel promotions.

**[Official]** Studio shows "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts. Compare it against your own channel's baseline.

**[Practitioner]** Hook in the first 1–2 seconds on screen and in audio, one idea per Short, large captions, a last line that loops back to the first, and a series structure.

#### 4. Emotional levers and the policy line

- **[Practitioner]** What makes people watch, rewatch and share: curiosity, surprise or awe, stakes, identity ("this is about people like me"), practical value, humor, nostalgia.
- **[Official, confirmed this session]** YouTube's monetization policy rewards "a unique creative voice" and "using AI to visualize a unique character and narrative you invented". It excludes content that is mass-produced, generic or repetitive, and "emotionally manipulative formulas" or content that "mimics existing formats ... to a degree that the videos feel interchangeable". Examples listed as off-putting include repeated scenarios of animals in distress, and videos that "stitch together unrelated or inconsistent AI clips to surprise or shock viewers".
- **[Inference]** Bandar Apna Dost's formula (the same characters in repeated melodramatic scenarios) sits close to this line. Its monetization status is unknown to me, so do not treat its views as proof the format is safe.
- **[Inference]** Use the levers that add real value (curiosity answered with sourced facts, a new angle, real stakes), not the ones that only manufacture emotion.

#### 5. Production levers, and what changes with AI visuals

- **[Practitioner]** Editing rhythm, sound design, music under narration, clear voice delivery, and captions all affect retention.
- **[Inference for our pipeline]**
  - **Where AI visuals help:** abstract or impossible subjects (space, deep time, microscopic or historical reconstructions, "what if" scenes). Nobody expects real footage there, so small imperfections matter less.
  - **Where they hurt:** close-up human faces and hands, readable text inside the picture, and anything that must look like real documentary footage.
  - **Consistency:** reuse one character reference and one style prefix, as the Video Generator already does for characters.
  - **Narration:** a synthetic voice with natural pacing; vary sentence length; avoid a monotone read. Test the voice by ear before committing.
  - **Mix of visuals:** use AI clips for the hook and key beats, then stills with motion, maps, charts and text cards for the rest, so each 8–15 second clip is spent where it counts.

#### 6. Experiment loop

- **[Official]** Studio gives: intro retention, key moments, "typical" comparisons against your last 10 similar-length videos, impressions and click-through rate, "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts, and **subscribers gained by content type**.
- **[Practitioner]** Test titles and thumbnails (Studio's "Test & Compare" is manual), read the retention graph, and change one thing at a time after a flop.
- **[Inference]** The pipeline can automate the reading: pull analytics, compare each video to the channel's own median, and feed the winners' title, hook and structure into the next plan.

#### 7. Implications for our plan (flagged, not yet applied)

1. **The earlier plan assumed Shorts would feed long-form viewers.** YouTube says that is uncommon, and the data fits: Bandar Apna Dost is Shorts-only, Khooni Monday's Shorts are about 5% of its long-form views. **Treat Shorts and long-form as separate products** with separate success measures, and check "subscribers gained by content type" in Studio.
2. **Shorts alone do not monetize below 10M views per 90 days** (section 2 of the niche research). A Shorts-only channel needs very large volume, so it is a reach strategy, not an income plan, until the announced incentive programs are published.
3. **Long-form carries the watch-hours route.** The measured sweet spot is 12–24 minute single-subject videos.
4. **Volume is not the answer.** The best long-form channels publish rarely. Experiments should differ in topic and structure, which is also what the policy wants.

#### 8. Blueprint for the scene-plan template (what Claude writes per video)

##### Long-form (12–24 min)
1. `promise`: one sentence the video must deliver; `titles` (3 options, written before the script); `thumbnail_concepts` (2): one focal subject, 3–5 words, high contrast.
2. `hook` (0–30 s): restate the promise within about 8 seconds, show the single most striking visual, state the stakes, end on an open question. No intro bumper.
3. `beats[]` of 45–90 s each: `claim`, `evidence_or_source`, `visual` (clip type: ai_video / still_motion / map / chart / card), `open_loop_opened`, `open_loop_closed`.
4. Rules: at least one open loop open at all times; a re-hook around one-third and two-thirds; the strongest moment in the first third; a new visual at least every 8 seconds; a source for every factual claim.
5. `payoff` that delivers the promise, plus `next_video_bridge`; no long outro.
6. Metadata: description with sources and chapters, tags, `containsSyntheticMedia` when footage looks real.

##### Short (20–45 s)
1. `hook_line` on screen and spoken in the first 1–2 seconds; one idea; 3–5 clips of 8 seconds or stills with motion.
2. `loop`: the last line connects back to the first. Large captions. Series or recurring-character tag if applicable.
3. No channel promotions as Shorts.

##### Pre-publish QA checklist
- The title and thumbnail promise is delivered within the first 30 seconds.
- Hook has a visual and an open question; no slow intro.
- Each beat has a source; no unsourced numbers or names.
- No close-up faces or readable in-picture text from AI clips unless checked frame by frame.
- Not a near-copy of the last 20 videos in structure and topic.
- Not built on distress, shock or a manufactured emotional formula.
- Synthetic-media label set where realistic.

##### Failure modes seen in the data
- Videos about the channel itself, announcements and format experiments (lowest views in every channel measured).
- Compilations and very long podcast-style uploads of otherwise good material.
- A rigid template with thin new content (policy risk) and repeated melodrama with the same characters (policy risk).
- Large subscriber counts with collapsed current views (HINDU STORIES 1.18M subs, 4.6K median views).

##### Metrics to watch (all thresholds are rules of thumb, not YouTube numbers)
- **Intro retention (30 s)**: compare to the channel's own "typical" band in Studio.
- **Click-through rate**: compare to the channel's own median; investigate large drops after a packaging change.
- **Average view duration and the retention curve shape**: look for early cliffs and late dips.
- **Shorts viewed vs swiped away**: compare to the channel's own baseline.
- **Subscribers gained by format**: shows whether Shorts or long-form builds the audience.

#### 2.1 Packaging (title, thumbnail, promise)

##### What YouTube says [Official]
- Test & Compare lets you test up to 3 titles and 3 thumbnails. YouTube picks the winner by **watch-time share, not click-through rate**, and warns that third-party tools which optimise click-through alone may pick a different winner.
- Results take a few days up to 2 weeks. Tests finish faster when the options differ more and when there are more impressions. YouTube suggests testing older videos first.
- Titles must be accurate: if the video does not match, viewers leave and discoverability suffers. Thumbnails should be uncluttered and as large as possible, 16:9 for custom thumbnails.
- The Intro metric (viewers still watching after 30 seconds) is higher when the first 30 seconds match what the title and thumbnail promised.

##### What the data shows [Measured]
Across 1,141 uploads from 46 channels, title format barely matters inside a channel:

| Title feature | Median views vs channel norm | Videos above norm |
|---|---|---|
| 5 words or fewer / 6-9 / 10+ | 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 | 47% / 48% / 48% |
| Contains a number | 1.00 | 47% |
| Is a question | 1.00 | 50% |
| Starts with How or Why | 1.00 | 49% |
| ALL-CAPS word | 1.02 | 51% |
| Colon or dash | 1.04 | 52% |
| Stakes or superlative word (never, worst, secret, truth...) | 1.00 | 46% |
| First person (I, we, my) | 0.97 | 45% |

| Starts with What, Who, When, Where | 1.08 | 64% (only 33 videos, 18 channels: suggestive, not proven) |

- A "50% above norm" result means no effect. **Title tricks do not move views.** Only meta and announcement titles clearly lose.
- The Hindi/Urdu sample was too small to read (89 videos, 5 channels). Emoji was the only feature with a hint (1.17, 65% above, 20 videos), which is not enough to rely on.
- **Limits:** the sample is established channels, titles cannot capture the topic, and newer videos are still gaining views.

##### What this means [Inference]
- The earlier top-versus-bottom comparison inside channels shows what separates winners: a **specific subject plus a stake or surprise** ("AI Just Crossed the Terrifying Line - Now What?" 24M against "We Made A New Channel!" 2.2M). That is a topic and promise effect, not a wording trick.
- So spend effort on **choosing the topic and promise**, then write several clearly different titles.

##### Thumbnails [Practitioner and vendor study, weak]
- A vidIQ study of 500 breakout thumbnails found faces in 69% (80% of the top 50), high contrast in 56% (not rising with success), text in 72% with a median of 5 words, and no text in 28%. vidIQ sells thumbnail tools, so treat this with caution.
- A separate data report covered by Search Engine Journal found face and no-face thumbnails perform similarly on average, with multi-face thumbnails doing best and the effect varying by niche.
- No controlled study isolating face, contrast or text length was found. The eye-tracking claim that faces draw the eye first is cited second-hand and I could not find the original.
- For a faceless channel, a face is not required: Kurzgesagt, Fall of Civilizations and Mr. Nightmare succeed without on-camera people.

##### Rules for the pipeline
1. Write the **promise** (one sentence the video delivers) before the script.
2. Produce **3 titles that differ in angle**, not in wording, because Test & Compare gives faster results when options differ more.
3. Produce 2-3 thumbnail concepts that differ visually: one focal subject, 3-5 words or none, high contrast, uncluttered.
4. The title and thumbnail must be delivered by the first 30 seconds.
5. Avoid meta titles ("update", "trailer", "compilation") for core content.
6. After publishing, run Test & Compare and judge by watch time, not click-through.

#### 2.3 Keeping people watching (beats, open loops, re-hooks)

##### What YouTube says [Official]
- The retention report shows flat parts (watched start to finish), gradual declines, **spikes** (rewatching or sharing) and **dips** (skipping or leaving). "Top moments" are where almost nobody left.
- Videos generally taper off during playback. If your top moments come late, **introduce the compelling content earlier**. Make more content like your top moments, and review dips to see why interest dropped.
- You can compare against typical retention for your last 10 videos of similar length.
- No ideal length: avoid filler and artificial stretching.

##### Why open loops work [Study]
- Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity (1994): curiosity arises when you notice a gap between what you know and what you want to know, and it feels like a deprivation you want to resolve. It needs some prior knowledge to notice the gap.
- Practical advice from a PBS/NOVA article drawing on it: start with a genuinely interesting question that opens a gap instead of giving the answer, and "prime the pump" with intriguing but incomplete information.
- Limits: rival accounts say curiosity peaks for novelty, and not every knowledge gap creates curiosity. The theory comes from lab and survey work, and **its application to video retention is an inference, not a tested result**.

##### Editing and pacing [Practitioner, contradictory]
- Pattern interrupts (B-roll, graphics, zooms, music change, angle change) are the common tool. Recommended frequency ranges from every 10-15 seconds to every 30-60 seconds depending on the source.
- A single creator's controlled test found over-editing causes viewer fatigue, and B-roll helps only when it directly illustrates what is being said. Anecdotal.
- A vendor analysis of 100 long-form channels says top-quartile channels hold viewers 20-30% longer (methodology unchecked). Targets of about 50% average percentage viewed circulate widely and vary with video length.
- Coverage of a leaked internal MrBeast production guide says it treats the first minute as the most important and teaches "retention editing". The guide's origin is unconfirmed, and its creator says to take it with a grain of salt.
- **Nothing here could be measured from public data**, because retention graphs are private to each channel.

##### Correction to my earlier blueprint [Inference]
My blueprint said "a new visual at least every 8 seconds". The sources support a **purposeful change every 10-30 seconds**, not constant cutting, and warn about fatigue. The rule should be: change the visual when the idea changes.

##### Rules for the pipeline
1. Split the video into **beats of 45-90 seconds**, each with one claim, its evidence or source, and its visual.
2. A visual change at each beat start and when the idea shifts inside a beat; roughly every 10-30 seconds; every visual must illustrate the line being spoken.
3. Keep **one to three open loops running** at a time. Every opened loop is closed before the end, and the biggest is closed last.
4. Re-hook (restate the promise or open a new question) near one-third and two-thirds of the video. These positions are starting hypotheses, not proven numbers.
5. Place the strongest moment in the first third.
6. After publishing, read the retention graph: fix the largest dip first, and copy what created the spikes.

##### What YouTube says [Official]

##### What the data shows [Measured]
1,141 uploads, 46 channels, views divided by each channel's own median.

| Length | Videos | Channels | Median vs channel norm | Videos above norm |

##### Format patterns seen on real channels [Measured, small]
- A strict repeatable title format gives steady views (Mr. Nightmare: "3 Scary TRUE ... Stories", 650K-1.1M each, 23-29 minutes).
- Core episodes beat compilations and podcasts on the same channel (Khooni Monday: 11-12 minute episodes 1.1-1.6M; 47-minute compilation about 1.2M; 76-minute podcast 248K).
- Landmark videos can be very long: Fall of Civilizations makes 4-7 hour documentaries (47 videos in five years, 2.2M median).
- **Policy caution:** a rigid template with thin new content is what YouTube's "generic or repetitive" category targets. The format can repeat; the content must be new and sourced each time.

##### Watch hours [Inference, assumed viewing time]
Views needed for the 8,000-hour entry bar: about 107K for a 10-minute video, 48K for 25 minutes, 19K for one hour. Longer videos need far fewer views but cost more footage.

##### Production load [Inference]
Veoframe Pro quota is 120 eight-second clips per day. A 30-minute video that is 40% AI clips needs about 90 clips, so roughly one such video per day at most. Stills with motion, maps and charts carry the rest.

##### Rules for the pipeline
1. Default to **20-40 minutes** for core videos; allow 10-20 minutes for simple topics; avoid under 5 minutes for long-form.
2. Let the topic set the length. Do not pad (official guidance).
3. Keep a repeatable title and episode format, with different content each time.
4. Avoid compilations and podcast formats as core output.
5. Budget AI clips per video against the daily quota.

#### 2.5 Ending

##### What YouTube says [Official]
- End screens can be placed in the last 5-20 seconds of a video that is at least 25 seconds long. Up to four elements. Viewers can hide them. Cards are hidden while an end screen shows.
- YouTube advises keeping overlays from covering visuals viewers want to see, and checking the "End screen element click rate" in Analytics.
- For binge watching, YouTube's TV tips suggest grouping episodes into playlists with a consistent title format.
- Viewers keep watching a session until a "Continue watching?" prompt appears (after 30 minutes on mobile, 60 on web, 180 on TV of uninterrupted autoplay).

##### What sources say [Practitioner]
- Deliver the promised payoff, then bridge to a related video. Cut long outros. No source measured this.

##### Inference
Retention tapers over the video (official), so by the end only the most engaged viewers remain. The ending matters less for reach and more for the next video and subscribers.

##### Rules for the pipeline
1. Deliver the **payoff the title promised** before anything else.
2. A **10-20 second bridge**: name one specific next video and why it follows.
3. No long sign-off, thanks or recap beyond one line.
4. Keep the final visual clean for the end screen; place 1-2 elements (best next video, subscribe) in the last 5-20 seconds.
5. Put episodes in playlists with a consistent title format.
6. Review end-screen click rate and the final retention segment after publishing.

#### Deeper research: what top creators and researchers have shared

Written sources only (interview transcripts, creator blogs, published research). Nothing here was checked inside a video.

##### Colin & Samir interview with MrBeast [Creator-stated, transcript read]
- **Re-engage halfway.** "It's very hard with a single storyline if you're doing a double-digit-minute video to have that one thing drip their entire attention... typically if you're doing a longer video, you should introduce a side story and have some plan halfway through to re-engage them." A single chase cannot fill 15 minutes.
- **Count what happens in the first 30 seconds.** Samir described writing down a video's first 30 seconds and finding more than 12 scene changes, new information about every six seconds, and a twist by 40 seconds, with tension running throughout.
- **Content over packaging.** "If you're frantically changing thumbnails afterwards, you're already way too far gone." They only publish when it feels like "a banger", and treat the title and thumbnail as a test of whether the video can back up its promise.
- **Making the title and thumbnail first** forces you to clarify the core premise (from The Futur's summary of the same interview).
- Colin & Samir's playbook summarises their three rules as: if they don't click, they don't watch; respect their time; do you want more stuff like this. They also say they track first-seven-second and first-30-second viewership. [Playbook page and a second-hand summary]

##### Story mechanics [Creator-stated and Study]
- **"But / therefore" instead of "and then"** (Trey Parker and Matt Stone, NYU 2014). Write the beats as a list and read the gaps. Wherever the link is only "and then", a viewer can leave without missing anything. Creators call this the single most useful pass for catching retention dips before editing.
- **Open loops and the Zeigarnik effect.** Zeigarnik's 1927 work found people remember unfinished tasks better than finished ones; creators use this to explain why an early question keeps viewers watching. Advanced creators stack loops so something is always pending. The step from lab result to video retention is an inference.
- **Information-gap theory** (Loewenstein, 1994): curiosity comes from noticing a gap between what you know and want to know, so a good hook gives enough to see the gap without the answer.
- **Sharing psychology (Berger's STEPPS):** social currency, triggers, emotion, public visibility, practical value, stories. Berger notes strongly sad content is shared less than average, even if remembered. Applies to share-driven growth, not retention.

##### How a top explainer channel actually works [Creator-stated, second-hand summaries]
- Kurzgesagt says a roughly 10-minute video takes at least 1,200 hours: reading books and papers and consulting experts first, scripts rewritten many times and reviewed by outside experts, then storyboarding, several weeks of illustration and animation, and narration recorded last. The script is the bottleneck. This is the opposite of an automated pipeline, so the lesson is that **research and a rewritten script, not visuals, are where quality comes from**.

##### Published research on video length [Study]
- A study of 6.9 million edX course-video sessions (Guo, Kim and Rubin, 2014) found engagement dropped sharply after about six minutes. That is educational lecture video, not YouTube entertainment, and conflicts with the 30-60 minute result in my measured data above. **Do not apply the six-minute figure to YouTube documentaries.** It does support keeping each beat short and varied.

##### Updated rules from the deeper research
1. Add a **planned side story or new question at the halfway point** (MrBeast).
2. In the first 30 seconds, aim for **frequent new information** (about one new piece every 6 seconds in his example) and a **twist or escalation by about 40 seconds**. This describes one famous channel, not a law.
3. Run the **"but / therefore" check on the beat list before any visuals are made**; rewrite any beat linked only by "and then".
4. Treat **research and the script as the main quality investment**; Kurzgesagt's own hours are spent there.
5. Decide whether to publish with the gut-check from the interview: if you would not call it a banger, rework it.

#### 3.2 One idea (20-45 seconds)

##### What YouTube says [Official]
- Shorts can be up to 3 minutes. The Shorts feed may weigh recency more, which helps new content get discovered.
- Studio shows "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts. Compare it against your own channel's baseline.
- In a YouTube blog interview (Aug 2025), creator Jordan Howlett said his best signal is the early reaction: "I usually know within the first hour if the video is going to do well", judged by the ratio of likes to views. He also said trends are a launchpad for new creators, but original ideas last longer.

##### What top Shorts creators say [Creator-stated]
From the Creator Science podcast interview with Jenny Hoyos (an 18-year-old creator the show says is averaging about 10M views per Short):
- **A good Short has a strong hook, one that could work as the title and thumbnail of a long video and still get clicks.** It should be visual enough to understand without sound, and so simple you could explain it to a five-year-old.
- **She checks hook wording in a readability checker** and aims for fifth-grade level or lower.
- **A good Short pushes you to the end and makes you rewatch.** She says 90%+ average retention is "the bare minimum" for a Short to blow up, and that this level requires viewers to rewatch. Her own average is about 95% on videos with around 10M views.
- **Length:** her own analysis says her most popular videos are about 34 seconds, so she aims for that. She stresses that everyone's number is different and has to be analysed per channel.
- **Process:** a rough script before filming, only to estimate length from the word count. She always writes a hook, then a **foreshadow** of two lines (about 3 seconds or less), then the body. Ideas are narrowed from a long list to about 25, then to about 10 with her editor, who acts as a strategist.
- **Closing rule:** "Whatever you say you're gonna do, you end it right after you do it." Nothing trails after the payoff.
- She sketches the hook as if it were a long-form thumbnail before deciding the words.

##### What guides and newsletters say [Practitioner, weak]
- Common four-part model: hook, core, payoff, then loop or call to action. Without a payoff the video feels unfinished.
- One guide: plan the opening three seconds and the final three seconds first, and treat everything between as a bridge. If the ending cannot answer the first question, the idea is probably a topic, not a story.
- Length advice conflicts: 15-25 s for tutorials, 20-40 s as a "safe default", 25-35 s for most viral Shorts. One vendor analysis claims only 2% of Shorts view variation correlates with length. Choose length by goal, not by rule.
- **"But / therefore" rule:** Trey Parker and Matt Stone (South Park) told an NYU audience in 2014 that if the link between two beats is "and then", something is wrong; it should be "but" (conflict) or "therefore" (consequence). Creators apply it to scripts, and Hoyos is reported to use it in her middle section.
- Colin & Samir's paid playbook names a "Stop, Hook, Payoff" method for Shorts from a guest creator. Its contents are behind a paywall, so I cannot describe it.

##### My measured notes [Measured, earlier work]
- The strongest Shorts titles in the channels studied were one-line curiosity questions or story hooks. Channel promotions got the least.

##### Rules for the pipeline [Inference from the sources]
1. **One promise per Short**, stated in one sentence a child could repeat.
2. Write the **hook visual first**, then the words. Check the hook text for reading level.
3. Structure: **hook (about 1-3 s), foreshadow (2 lines, about 3 s), body linked by "but" and "therefore", payoff in the last seconds, then stop.**
4. Start with **25-40 seconds** and let the channel's own analytics set the final length.
5. For AI clips: a 30-40 second Short needs about 3-5 clips of 8 seconds, or fewer clips plus stills with motion.
6. Compare each Short's "viewed vs swiped away" with the channel's own baseline.

#### 3.3 Loop ending

##### What YouTube says [Official]
- A Shorts view counts each time it starts to play or replays, with no minimum watch time (a change dated Mar 31, 2025). A later help page says views are counted the moment a video starts to play in all formats from Aug 24, 2026. The two pages are worded differently, so check the live page if this matters.
- **"Engaged views" do not include loops.** Partner Program eligibility and Shorts earnings use engaged views, not raw views.
- So a loop can raise public view counts without raising what counts for pay. [Inference from the two official facts above.]

##### What creators say [Creator-stated and Practitioner]
- Hoyos links rewatching to retention above 90%. Her tactic is to **give the viewer an expectation, close it, then add a twist** (example she gave: she shows she will give someone a gift, then the gift breaks).
- vidIQ describes a seamless loop as hiding the join between the last frame and the first. A brand guide suggests filming the ending shot first and the opening shot to match it.
- Cautions in the same sources: looping does not guarantee double watch time, and over-trimming a Short to two or three seconds rarely builds real watch time.
- Another source warns that an open curiosity loop with no payoff earns swipe-backs and "not interested" taps, not satisfaction.

##### Rules for the pipeline [Inference]
1. **Payoff first, loop second.** The viewer must get what the hook promised before any loop trick.
2. Make the **last line or frame connect to the first** so a replay feels natural, but do not delay the payoff to do it.
3. Do not rely on loops for income: engaged views exclude them.
4. Track average percentage viewed. Values above 100% mean rewatching.
5. Avoid cliffhanger endings with no resolution; they are the "unsatisfying" kind of content that policy warns about.

#### 3.4 Recurring character or series

##### What YouTube says [Official]
- **Shorts series:** creators can organise Shorts into seasons and episodes with custom thumbnails and sequential playback; members can continue straight to the next episode.
- **Microdramas (YouTube blog, Oct 2026):** serialised vertical mini-series told in fast 60-second scenes passed **6.5 billion views in the first half of 2026**, with watch time growing more than 50% year over year. About **40% of views and 30% of clicks came more than 30 days after publishing**, so early episodes keep finding viewers. Examples named: Japan's GOKKO CLUB (emotional hook within 15 seconds, vertical-first framing) and India's Shorts Break (high-frequency publishing, universal themes, audience comments shaping future characters).
- For binge watching, YouTube's TV tips suggest playlists and a consistent title format per episode.
- **Policy line [Official, monetization policy page]:** content that puts "characters in the same situation over and over again with the same outcome" is listed as repetitive and ineligible, while using AI "to visualize a unique character and narrative you invented" is listed as acceptable. A recurring character is fine; a repeated identical scenario is not.

##### What creators and guides say
- **Neural Viz [secondary summaries of a WIRED profile, not the creator's own words]:** writes scripts by hand and storyboards shots, uses several tools to keep lighting and sight lines consistent, performs the characters himself using motion capture ("wearing AI as a mask"), and builds multiple series in one shared world.
- A creator-strategy guide frames a series as **character, quest, tension and a repeatable format**. It notes comedic character series suffer format fatigue faster than explainer series, and that the real metric is whether viewers return, not raw views.
- Another guide says to make the one recurring element a person (a character), not a product shot. Marketing blogs; no data.

##### My measured notes [Measured, earlier work]
- Bandar Apna Dost (AI-made) uses named recurring characters and melodrama and has Shorts up to 144M views, but its worst Shorts got about 48K, a 3,000-times spread, and its policy exposure is high.
- In long-form titles across 46 channels, videos marked as series parts ("Part 2", "Ep 12") did slightly **worse** than the same channels' other videos (median 0.87 of channel norm, 33 videos, 8 channels). Later episodes of a series often get fewer views than the first. Series help return viewers, not each video's views.

##### Rules for the pipeline [Inference from the sources]
1. Build a **character bible** (look, voice, goal, flaw) and a **world bible** that every episode reuses, so visuals stay consistent.
2. Each episode must **change the situation**: new problem, new "but", new consequence. Never repeat the same scenario with the same outcome.
3. Plan arcs across episodes (seasons) and keep one repeatable format and title pattern.
4. Use YouTube's Shorts series feature or playlists, and number episodes consistently.
5. Track **returning viewers and subscribers gained** per Short, not just views.
6. Treat recurring-character comedy as the highest-risk format for format fatigue and for the repetitive-content policy.

#### 4. Hook anatomy by format

All timings are [G] unless marked. No official source gives a second-by-second structure.

##### Long-form, 0 to 30 seconds

Official anchors [O]: grab attention by what you say or show, make the topic clear early, tease the rest, branding after the hook. The Intro metric is measured at 30 seconds.

| Time | Say | Show | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | The most striking, specific line from the video. No greeting. | The strongest visual, already moving | A greeting such as "Hey guys, welcome back" is called a retention killer by one blog [B] |
| 3-10 s | Deliver the title's promise in one sentence | A visual that matches the title and thumbnail | Intro success is tied to matching expectations [O] |
| 10-20 s | Add stakes or the first open loop (a question or a "but") | A change of image or motion | Blogs suggest teasing a later payoff [B] |
| 20-30 s | Bridge into the body and say what the viewer gets; foreshadow | The first body scene | Hoyos calls foreshadowing "the most underrated thing in hooks" [C, summary page] |

#### 5. What changes with AI visuals and synthetic voice

**Policy first [O].** YouTube's monetization page renamed "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content" and lists as non-monetizable:
- "Videos that use a highly similar storyline template across multiple videos."
- "Image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal narrative or educational value."
- "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates that gives the impression of mass production."

It allows "the same intro and outro across videos, if the bulk of each video differs."

Implication [G]: **rotate hook formulas and structure.** Never output the same hook skeleton with swapped nouns every time. Official wording on how YouTube treats AI voice specifically was not found.

**Visual choices**
- An impossible or cinematic image suits Veo-style clips as a cold open, because AI can show what cameras cannot. The Playbook supports a "stunning visual" opening [O].
- Google's Veo best-practice documentation [O]: "dedicate each prompt to a single, focused moment"; chaining events "often leads to muddled or incomplete videos"; for character consistency, build "a rich set of unchangeable features"; avoid quotation marks, which can trigger text rendering.
- Text artifacts: generate clips without text and add on-screen text in the editor [G]. How bad text artifacts are in current Veo versions: [U].
- Faces: no official guidance found [U]. A cautious rule: avoid close-up faces, hands and crowds in the first 3 seconds, where artifacts would show most [G].
- Match the hook image to the title. A mismatch directly harms Intro [O].

**Synthetic voice (ElevenLabs documentation, one vendor only [O])**
- Punctuation affects delivery; ellipses add pauses and weight.
- Break tags up to 3 seconds; too many can cause instability.
- Speed range 0.7 to 1.2.
- Expand abbreviations and write numbers in spoken form.
- Other TTS engines differ [U].

Hook-line writing rules for TTS [G]: one idea per sentence, under about 12 words; the key noun early; one comma-pause before the reveal; no numerals the engine may misread.

**Urdu and Hindi**
- The ElevenLabs page has no Hindi or Urdu guidance.
- A Pakistan guide [B, single source, no data] claims Nastaliq thumbnails suit local audiences and tags should include Roman Urdu. Untested.
- **No sourced Hindi or Urdu hook examples or retention data were found.** A Hindi template the search surfaced, "Kya aap jaante hain ki [fact]?", was the search tool's own suggestion, not from a source. Use it only as a hypothesis to test [U].
- Keep Hindi (Devanagari) and Urdu (Nastaliq) libraries separate [G]. Preferred caption script and mixing ratio: [U].

#### Pre-publish hook checklist
1. Does the first line contain a specific claim, number, scene or question, not a greeting?
2. Does the first visual match the title and thumbnail? [O]
3. Is the title's promise stated within about 10 seconds (long-form) or in the first line (Shorts)? [G]
4. Is there one open loop, and is it paid off?
5. Is branding after the hook? [O]
6. Does the Short make sense on mute? [C]
7. Are AI visuals free of text, face and hand artifacts in the first 3 seconds? [G]
8. Is the narration line short enough for TTS to land the key noun early? [G]
9. Is this hook formula different from the last several videos' hooks (policy risk)? [O/G]
10. Is every number or claim verified? [G]

#### Failure modes
- Title and hook mismatch, which lowers Intro [O].
- Buried lede: the point arrives after the viewer has left.
- Fake or unresolved open loop.
- Flashy intro or logo first [O].
- An unverified statistic spoken with confidence by the narrator.
- The same hook template reused across videos [O].
- Text artifacts or distorted faces in the opening frame [G].
- A looping Short that inflates views but not engaged views [O].
